# Encore music glossary

## Shoegaze
Layers of distorted guitars, heavy reverb, and hazy vocals create a dense, dreamy sound. Use it when someone wants atmosphere more than clearly defined lyrics.

## Synthwave
Retro synthesizers, pulsing bass, and electronic drums evoke 1980s film soundtracks and video games. 

## Lo-fi hip hop
Relaxed hip hop beats blend with mellow samples, soft keyboards, and deliberately imperfect textures such as tape hiss. Rooted in instrumental hip-hop and sampling culture, it is good for studying, working, or winding down.

## Ambient
Atmospheric textures, sustained tones, and spacious arrangements take priority over strong beats or conventional song structures. It suits quiet concentration, relaxation, or unobtrusive background listening.

## Post-rock
Guitars, bass, and drums build expansive soundscapes, often with few vocals and gradual shifts from quiet passages to powerful climaxes. It's good for cinematic listening or work that benefits from instrumental music.

## Krautrock
Repetitive grooves, electronic experimentation, and psychedelic textures characterize this broad label for experimental West German rock. It suits listeners seeking hypnotic rhythms, adventurous sounds, or early influences on electronic music.

## Dream pop
Soft vocals, shimmering guitars, and atmospheric production create a floating, melodic sound. Emerging from alternative music in the 1980s, it suits gentle daydreaming, reflective evenings, or listeners who enjoy shoegaze but want less distortion.

## Trip-hop
Slow, heavy beats combine with deep bass, samples, and moody vocals or instrumental textures. It suits late-night listening or requests for something dark, relaxed, and atmospheric.

## Downtempo
Unhurried electronic beats and spacious arrangements produce a relaxed but rhythmic sound. Growing out of electronic and chillout scenes, it suits lounging, quiet conversation, or focus sessions that need more movement than ambient music.

## House
A steady four-on-the-floor kick drum supports repeating basslines, electronic textures, and often soulful vocals. Originating in Chicago clubs in the 1980s, it suits dancing, upbeat background listening, or steady-paced exercise.

## Techno
Repetitive electronic rhythms, synthesized sounds, and gradual changes emphasize momentum and texture. Originating in Detroit in the 1980s, it suits dancing, sustained workouts, or listeners seeking driving music with limited vocal distraction.

## Drum and bass
Fast breakbeats and prominent basslines create an energetic sound that can range from smooth and melodic to dark and abrasive. Emerging from the UK rave and jungle scenes in the early 1990s, it suits running, gaming, or high-energy listening.

## Funk
Syncopated basslines, tight drums, and rhythmic guitar or horn parts put the groove at the center. Developed by Black American musicians in the 1960s, it suits dancing, lifting the mood, or requests for lively, rhythm-focused music.

## Focus music
Music chosen to support concentration, often featuring limited vocals, predictable patterns, and restrained dynamic changes. This is a listening purpose rather than a genre; it can include ambient, classical, or instrumental beats depending on the listener’s preferences.

## Chill
An informal mood label for relaxed, low-pressure music, often with gentle rhythms and soft textures. Popular across lounge, electronic, and playlist culture, it suits unwinding, casual socializing, or keeping the atmosphere calm without necessarily making it sleepy.

## Melancholy
Music that conveys sadness, longing, or wistful reflection through melody, harmony, lyrics, or vocal delivery. A mood found across genres, it suits introspective listening or requests for music that matches a subdued emotional state.

## Hype or workout music
Music selected for excitement, motivation, or physical momentum, often using strong beats, energetic performances, and impactful climaxes. Drawn from genres such as hip-hop, rock, and electronic dance music, it suits training, pre-event preparation, or an energy boost.

## Uplifting
Music that feels hopeful, encouraging, or emotionally buoyant, whether gentle or high-energy. This cross-genre mood label suits a morning boost, a celebratory playlist, or a request for positivity without necessarily demanding fast tempos.

## Dark
Music with an ominous, brooding, or tense atmosphere, often created through heavy bass, dissonance, sparse textures, or unsettling lyrics. Used across many genres, it suits suspenseful settings or listeners seeking intensity rather than cheerfulness.

## Cinematic
Music that evokes scenes, stories, or dramatic movement through expansive arrangements, orchestration, or strong dynamic shifts. Influenced by film scoring but not limited to soundtracks, it suits imaginative work, immersive listening, or a sense of scale.

## B-side
Originally the reverse side of a vinyl single, usually containing a secondary track rather than the main promoted song. The term now also describes certain companion releases or rarities; someone might request B-sides to explore beyond an artist’s best-known material.

## EP
Short for “extended play,” an EP is a release generally longer than a single but shorter than an album, though platform definitions vary. The term comes from record formats and is useful when someone wants a compact introduction to an artist or a shorter, cohesive listening experience.

## Remaster
A version of an existing recording whose final audio has been processed again, potentially changing clarity, tonal balance, or loudness. Rooted in the mastering stage of record production, it is useful when comparing editions; a remaster is not necessarily a new performance, a new mix, or an improvement.

## Cover
A new performance or recording of a song previously recorded by another artist. Covers can closely follow the original or change its genre, arrangement, and mood, making them useful for listeners who want familiar songwriting with a different sound.

## Deep cut
A relatively obscure track in an artist’s catalog, often an album song that received little promotion. Coming from album-listening and radio culture, the term suits requests for overlooked favorites rather than hits; a deep cut does not have to be a B-side or rare release.

## Remix
A version of an existing recording that changes elements such as its arrangement, beat, tempo, or effects, often using parts of the original audio. Closely associated with dub and dance music traditions, remixes suit requests for a familiar track adapted to another mood or setting.

## Live recording
A recording of a performance, often featuring audience noise, altered arrangements, or more spontaneous playing than a studio version. It suits listeners who want concert atmosphere, extended solos, or a different interpretation of familiar songs.

## Instrumental
Music without sung lyrics, typically placing melodies and musical development in instruments or electronic sounds. Found across musical traditions, it suits listeners who want to avoid lyrical distraction, though instrumental music can still be intense or attention-grabbing.

## BPM
Short for “beats per minute,” BPM measures musical tempo rather than mood or overall intensity. Used in performance, production, and DJing, it helps match music to movement or pacing, although two tracks with the same BPM can feel very different.

## Acoustic
Music performed primarily with acoustic instruments, often with a more exposed or stripped-down arrangement than an electric version. Common in folk traditions and unplugged performances, it suits requests for an intimate or organic sound, though acoustic music is not always quiet or slow.
